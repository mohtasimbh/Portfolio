I've tried them all. Evernote, back when it was the default answer to "where should I keep notes?" Notion, seduced by its databases and beautiful templates. Roam Research, convinced that networked thought was the future. Apple Notes for simplicity. Google Docs for collaboration. Paper notebooks for that analog feeling.

Each had something to recommend it. Each eventually frustrated me. Evernote became bloated and expensive. Notion's complexity became its own overhead. Roam's subscription felt extractive for something so personal. The simpler tools lacked features I needed; the complex ones had features I didn't want.

Then I found Obsidian, and I stopped looking.

From research notes to daily journaling to project management to this very blog post, Obsidian handles it all. Not because it does everything, but because it does the right things — and gets out of the way for everything else. Here's why it works.

Local-First Philosophy
======

My notes are plain Markdown files. They live in a folder on my computer. No proprietary format, no cloud dependency, no subscription fee, no authentication required.

This matters more than you might think.

**Longevity.** I've lost notes to discontinued services. Apps I depended on were acquired, pivoted, or shut down. The notes I wrote in 2015 should still be accessible in 2035. Markdown files will be readable in fifty years — they're plain text with simple formatting. Try saying that about .notion exports or Evernote's .enex format.

**Ownership.** With cloud-first apps, your notes live on someone else's server. You're trusting that company to maintain access, respect your privacy, and not change terms unexpectedly. With Obsidian, my notes are files I own. I can back them up however I want, version control them with Git, encrypt them, sync them via any method.

**Speed.** Local files are fast. No waiting for sync, no spinning wheels while the cloud catches up, no degraded experience on airplane mode. Open Obsidian, and your notes are there instantly. Search returns results immediately. The responsiveness is addictive.

**Privacy.** My notes contain ideas, personal reflections, project plans, passwords (encrypted), financial thoughts. They never leave my computer unless I choose to sync them. No company is training AI models on my private thoughts.

**Resilience.** If Obsidian disappeared tomorrow — company goes bankrupt, developers move on, whatever — I'd lose a nice interface but none of my actual content. The notes would still be Markdown files, readable in any text editor. I'd miss features, but I wouldn't lose data.

The cloud isn't evil, but it's a tradeoff. For something as personal as a note-taking system, I want the control that local-first provides.

Linking Ideas: The Network Effect
======

Obsidian's core innovation is bidirectional linking. Create a link to another note with [[double brackets]]. That note now shows a backlink to the original. Ideas connect naturally.

This changes how you organize. Traditional note apps are hierarchical: folders within folders within folders. Finding something means remembering where you put it, tracing the same path you took to store it.

Obsidian is networked. Notes link to notes link to notes. A concept mentioned in three different contexts links to all three. The relationships are explicit, traversable, growing.

**Example:** I have a note called "Confirmation Bias." It's linked from my notes on research methodology, cognitive psychology, media criticism, personal decision-making, and a specific project analyzing survey responses. When I open that note, I see all those contexts in the backlinks panel. The concept appears wherever it's relevant.

**Emergent structure.** I don't have to decide the perfect category when I create a note. I add links as connections occur to me. Over time, structure emerges from the links themselves. Clusters of related notes become visible without imposed hierarchy.

**Resurfacing old ideas.** In traditional systems, old notes disappear into archives. In Obsidian, linked notes resurface whenever relevant. I open a project note and see backlinks to forgotten papers I read months ago. Past thinking informs present work.

**The graph view.** Obsidian visualizes your notes as a network — nodes and edges showing how your ideas connect. It's not just pretty; it's useful. Clusters emerge, showing topic groups. Orphaned notes stand out, prompting you to integrate them. Densely connected notes reveal what you think about most.

This approach mirrors how thinking actually works. Ideas don't file neatly into categories. They connect across domains, resurface unexpectedly, combine into new insights. Obsidian's structure enables that kind of emergent creativity in a way hierarchical systems can't.

Daily Notes: The Foundation
======

Every day starts with a daily note, automatically titled with today's date. It takes five minutes to create and becomes invaluable over time.

**What goes in a daily note:**

*What happened yesterday.* Not everything — the highlights, the notable moments, what I want to remember.

*What's planned today.* Tasks, meetings, intentions. Not a detailed schedule; a general orientation.

*What's on my mind.* Ideas, worries, observations. The random thoughts that don't have another home yet.

*Links outward.* If I mention a project, I link to its note. If I reference a person, I link to their note. These links create a web connecting my daily experience to my broader knowledge.

**Why daily notes matter:**

*Temporal context.* When did I think that? When did I meet that person? Search my daily notes.

*Journaling without pressure.* I'm not trying to write beautifully. I'm capturing what happened. Low stakes, high value.

*Thinking in writing.* The morning brain dump clears mental clutter. Getting thoughts on paper makes them tractable.

*Continuity.* Each day links to the previous and next. I can scroll back through time, seeing patterns I couldn't notice in the moment.

I've been doing daily notes for two years. It's the single practice that made Obsidian stick. The habit is simple; the accumulated value is enormous.

Plugins: Extending the Core
======

Obsidian's base functionality is intentionally minimal. Plugins add what you need, and the community has created thousands.

**Dataview: Notes as a database**

Turn your vault into a queryable database. "Show me all notes tagged #project created in the last month." "List all people notes with a 'company' field of 'Acme'." "Display my book notes sorted by rating."

I use Dataview for:
- *Project dashboards* showing all tasks across notes
- *Reading lists* pulling from my book notes
- *Weekly reviews* aggregating the week's daily notes
- *CRM-style views* of people I've met

The queries are written in a SQL-like syntax (or JavaScript for complex cases). There's a learning curve, but the payoff is transformative.

**Templater: Sophisticated templates**

Go beyond simple text replacement. Templater supports variables, dates, prompts, and JavaScript.

My daily note template:
- Auto-populates today's date in the title
- Links to yesterday's note
- Pulls calendar events from a file
- Creates sections for todos, journal, and ideas

My literature note template:
- Prompts for author, title, year
- Creates the filename in a consistent format
- Adds standard sections (summary, key ideas, quotes, connections)
- Tags it for processing

Templates reduce friction. Creating a new note of any type takes seconds, formatted consistently.

**Calendar: Visual navigation**

A simple calendar view linked to daily notes. Click a date, create or open that day's note. See which days have notes (dots appear). Navigate weeks and months visually.

For reviewing past notes, it's invaluable. "What was I thinking in March?" Click through the days.

**Kanban: Project management**

Trello-style boards inside Obsidian. Each card is a note (or links to one). Drag cards between columns.

I manage writing projects this way. Columns for "Ideas," "Drafting," "Editing," "Done." Each card is a piece I'm working on. The board lives alongside my notes, not in a separate app.

**Zotero Integration: Literature workflow**

Pull references from your Zotero library. Create literature notes with metadata pre-filled. Import highlights and annotations. The research workflow becomes seamless.

When I read a paper, I highlight in my PDF reader, Zotero captures the annotations, and a plugin imports them into Obsidian as a literature note. My reading and thinking happen in one place.

**Other plugins I use:**

- *QuickAdd:* Rapid note creation with templates
- *Periodic Notes:* Weekly and monthly notes alongside daily
- *Admonition:* Callout boxes for warnings, tips, notes
- *Excalidraw:* Sketching and diagramming inside Obsidian
- *Tasks:* Enhanced task management with due dates and queries

The plugin ecosystem means Obsidian grows with you. Start simple, add complexity as needed.

My Workflows
======

**Capturing fleeting thoughts:**

Quick capture is crucial. When an idea strikes, I need to write it down before it disappears.

On desktop: A hotkey opens a quick-entry box. Type the thought, hit enter, it saves to my inbox note.

On mobile: Open the app, hit the daily note button, type into today's entry.

Later, during processing, I review the inbox and move items to appropriate notes.

**Processing literature:**

1. Save paper to Zotero (browser extension)
2. Read and highlight (in PDF reader)
3. Import highlights to Obsidian (Zotero plugin)
4. Write summary in my own words
5. Add links to related notes and concepts
6. Tag with relevant project tags

Each paper becomes a node in my knowledge network, connected to everything relevant.

**Writing projects:**

1. Create a project note (template with standard sections)
2. Add it to the Kanban board
3. Link to relevant research notes
4. Outline in the project note
5. Draft in a separate note, linked
6. Move through Kanban columns as I progress

Everything is linked. Opening the project note shows all the research, drafts, and related ideas.

**Weekly review:**

Every Sunday, I create a weekly note (template via Periodic Notes). It prompts me to:
- Review the week's daily notes
- Note accomplishments and progress
- Identify what's stuck
- Set intentions for next week

Dataview queries show tasks completed, notes created, projects touched. The review surfaces patterns I'd miss day-to-day.

The Philosophy of It
======

Beyond practical benefits, Obsidian changed my relationship with thinking.

**Externalized memory.** I don't try to remember everything. I write it down. My brain holds ideas loosely, knowing they're captured in notes. This frees mental space for creativity and connection rather than retention.

**Cumulative knowledge.** Every note I write adds to a growing knowledge base. Links connect new ideas to old. The value compounds. A note I write today might connect to something from three years ago, creating new understanding.

**Thinking in writing.** The act of writing is the act of thinking. Fuzzy ideas become clear when forced into words. Obsidian makes writing frictionless, which makes thinking more frequent.

**Serendipitous discovery.** Browsing through backlinks and the graph view, I encounter forgotten ideas. These chance rediscoveries spark new connections. The system surfaces things I didn't know I was looking for.

It's not about the tool, ultimately. It's about the practice of thinking in writing, of connecting ideas, of building external memory. Obsidian just removes the friction and gets out of the way.

That's the highest praise I can give.

Getting Started
======

If this sounds appealing, here's how to begin:

1. **Download Obsidian** (free for personal use). Create a vault.

2. **Start with daily notes.** Use the Daily Notes core plugin. Write something every day for two weeks.

3. **Add links liberally.** When you mention something that could be its own note, link to it with [[brackets]]. Don't worry if the note doesn't exist yet — Obsidian will create it when you click.

4. **Install a few plugins.** Calendar and Templater are good starting points. Don't install twenty plugins on day one.

5. **Develop your own system.** Other people's workflows are inspiration, not prescription. Adapt to what works for you.

The learning curve exists but isn't steep. Within a month, you'll have your own practices, your own templates, your own emerging knowledge network.

Welcome to the garden.
