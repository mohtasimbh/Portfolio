# The Ship Has Already Hit the Iceberg


Passengers of the Titanic actually didn't want to leave the ship for almost two hours after it hit the iceberg. People thought Titanic was unsinkable. Lifeboats left half empty. Only in the last half an hour did people start to panic, but by then it was already too late. Not enough lifeboats. Nowhere to hide. Nowhere to run. Now, I came here today to tell you that we find ourselves in a similar predicament, in a similar situation. Our ship has already hit the iceberg. 

We have already started to sink without even realizing it. And I'm talking about the ship of our personal freedoms. Personal freedoms have been eroded everywhere in the world, almost without exception. The strange thing about losing freedom is that it doesn't always feel like losing freedom. Nobody comes to your door and tells you, "Today, you are losing your freedom." It happens slowly. A new restriction appears. A little more surveillance becomes normal. A little less privacy is accepted. A new rule is introduced and we are told it's for our safety. We accept it because it doesn't affect us personally. Then another change comes, and we accept that too.

After a while, we look around and realize the world is different, but we don't remember exactly when it changed. This is where the Titanic story becomes important. The passengers trusted the ship because they believed it was safe. We sometimes make the same mistake with our freedoms. We look around and say, "I'm still free. I can speak. I can use the internet. I can live my life." So we assume everything is fine. But freedom isn't measured by whether you feel free today. You have to look at the direction things are moving. Think about privacy. People often say, "I have nothing to hide." But privacy isn't about hiding something wrong. You close your door even when you're doing nothing wrong. You don't want someone reading every message you send or following you everywhere you go. Privacy gives you space to think, communicate, disagree, and live without constant observation. Technology has made this issue even more important. Your phone knows where you go. Your searches create records. Your purchases create data. Your online activity tells companies and institutions a great deal about you. Technology itself isn't the enemy. The real question is who controls these systems, what information they collect, who has access to it, and what happens when those powers are abused. We keep waiting for the danger to become obvious. We say, "It's only one new rule." "It's only temporary." "It's only affecting those people." And then another rule comes. Another restriction. Another expansion of power. Another piece of privacy disappears. We adjust, and eventually the abnormal becomes normal.

That's how freedom can disappear without a single dramatic moment. So what happens when the rule you supported yesterday is used against you tomorrow? What happens when the institution you trusted is controlled by someone you don't trust? What happens when information collected for one purpose is used for another? That's why freedom needs principles. You shouldn't defend freedom only when it benefits you. You should defend it when it protects people you disagree with too, because the same system that protects their rights today might protect yours tomorrow. The Titanic had lifeboats, but there weren't enough. And some left before they were full. Having a solution isn't enough. You have to recognize the problem early enough to use it. The same is true for freedom. We have laws, courts, constitutions, journalists, elections, and people willing to speak up, but none of these things work automatically. They depend on people paying attention. They depend on people asking questions. They depend on someone saying, "Wait. Why are we doing this?" I'm not saying the world is ending. I'm saying we still have time. We should protect privacy before we desperately need it. We should defend free speech before our own speech is threatened. We should question government power before we personally experience its consequences. We should pay attention before the water reaches the deck. Maybe our ship hasn't sunk. Maybe it won't. Maybe we still have plenty of time. I hope we do. But I don't want to be one of those people sitting inside the ship saying, "Don't worry. Titanic is unsinkable." I would rather ask uncomfortable questions while there is still time. Because the worst moment to start caring about the lifeboats is when the ship is already underwater. The iceberg is already there. The question is whether we're going to pretend we don't see it, or whether we're going to do something while we still have time.
