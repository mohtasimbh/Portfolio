Last night at Iftar, I noticed something strange.

There were five minutes left before Maghrib. I sat and waited. Those five minutes felt long. Slow. Heavy. Each second moved like it carried weight.

Then I looked down from my balcony. I saw people rushing home. Some were walking fast. Some were on bikes. Some were almost running. They were trying to reach home before Iftar.

For them, those same five minutes looked different. Quick. Urgent. Almost gone.

That moment made me ask a simple question.

Is time constant?

What Physics Says

In everyday life, time is constant.

One minute equals sixty seconds. Your clock and my clock move at the same rate. If we stand in the same city, we share the same physical time.

Physics treats time as measurable and consistent.

There is one exception. In Albert Einstein’s theory of relativity, time changes with speed and gravity. Astronauts on the International Space Station age slightly slower than people on Earth. The difference is tiny, measured in milliseconds.

But during Iftar in our neighborhoods, physics is not bending time.

So what is happening?

What Your Brain Does to Time

Your brain does not measure time like a clock. It estimates time based on attention and emotion.

When you wait for something important:

• You focus on the clock
• You feel hunger
• You think about food
• You anticipate relief

Your mind tracks every second. Time feels longer.

When you rush toward something:

• You focus on reaching your destination
• You think about traffic and distance
• Your body moves fast
• Your attention stays busy

Your brain processes more action and less waiting. Time feels shorter.

Research in psychology shows this pattern clearly. Boredom stretches perceived time. High engagement compresses it. Strong emotions also distort it.

The Five Minute Experiment

Think about this:

Sit still and stare at a wall for five minutes. It feels long.

Talk with a close friend for five minutes. It feels short.

The clock does not change. Your mind does.

What I Learned From That Balcony

That small moment before Iftar showed me something practical.

Time is stable.
Your experience of it is not.

If you want time to feel faster, stay engaged. Move. Focus on a task.

If you want time to slow down, remove distractions. Pay attention. Be present.

You do not control the clock.
You influence how you experience it.

Those five minutes were the same for everyone in my neighborhood. But inside each person, they felt completely different.

Time stayed constant.
The mind did the rest.
